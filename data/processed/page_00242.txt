Healthy lungs look and feel like sponges. Theyâ re pink, squishy, and flexible enough to squeeze and expand with each breath. Their main job is to take oxygen out of the air you breathe and pass it into your blood.
When you inhale, air enters your body through your windpipe, or trachea, the tube that connects your mouth and nose with your lungs. The air then travels through bronchial tubes, which move air in and out of your lungs . All along your airways, mucus and hair-like structures called cilia get rid of dust and dirt that come in with the air. Air keeps moving through your airways until it reaches tiny balloon-like air sacs in your lungs called alveoli. From there, the oxygen moves into your blood.
When you exhale, your lungs remove carbon dioxide from your blood in a process called gas exchange.
Smoking throws this entire process out of balance.
A single puff of cigarette smoke has more than 7,000 chemicals, and almost 70 of them are known to cause cancer. When you breathe it in, these toxins go deep into your lungs and inflame them. Your airways start to make too much mucus. That leads to problems like coughing, bronchitis, and pneumonia .
Toxins make the tiny airways in your lungs swell. This can make your chest feel tight and can cause wheezing and shortness of breath. If you continue smoking, the inflammation can build into scar tissue, which makes it harder to breathe. Sticky tar from tobacco builds up inside your lungs too. After years of smoking, it can give them a black color.

The tubes that carry air from your windpipe to your lungs are called bronchi. When they become inflamed, your lungs produce extra mucus, which can cause breathing trouble or coughing as your body tries to clear it out. People with chronic bronchitis have symptoms that persist for two years or more. Chronic bronchitis is most common in smokers, since smoke irritates the bronchi and leads to long-term inflammation.Â
Emphysema is a lung disease that makes it hard to breathe. It happens when the alveoli become damaged. Normally, these sacs stretch to fill with air, helping oxygen move into your bloodstream. With emphysema, the alveoli weaken and can even break down over time. This damage stops them from expanding and contracting normally, trapping old air in the lungs and making it harder for fresh air to enter.Â
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD for short) is a group of long-term lung conditions that make it hard to breathe due to lung damage. It includes emphysema and chronic bronchitis. While these conditions affect different parts of your lungs, they share similar symptoms â mainly inflammation of the airways, which limits how much air your lungs can take in. They also usually occur together. Smoking is the most common cause of COPD, but long-term exposure to air pollution or chemicals can also play a role.Â
Pulmonary hypertension is a type of high blood pressure that affects the pulmonary arteries, which supply blood to your lungs. It happens when the walls of these arteries become thick and stiff, making it harder for blood to flow through them. This puts extra pressure on the right side of the heart, forcing it to work harder to pump blood into the lungs. Over time, this added strain can weaken the heart and may even lead to heart failure. Pulmonary hypertension is often a side effect of COPD. Quitting smoking lowers your chances of getting it.

Smoking affects more than just your lungs. When you inhale smoke, it travels down your airways and the chemicals are absorbed into your bloodstream, eventually reaching your brain and other organs. This can damage your bones, eyes, immune system, hormones and even your DNA, increasing your chances of having other types of cancer, heart disease, type 2 diabetes , stroke and fertility issues.
